Setup

Every run is one process (examples/experiment.rs) simulating the whole network on an in-process fake wire that carries the same code paths as the real UDP/TCP transports. Node addresses are drawn at random from 10.0.0.0/8 with random ports using the run's seed; node ids are SHA-256 of ip:port, so each seed gives a different topology. Values are 64 random bytes from the same seed. Lookups are counted only in the measure phase, after the network is built and the values stored.

RPC policy: datagram RPCs (PING, FIND_NODE) are resent every 200 ms of silence, at most 5 sends, so a call gives up after 1000 ms. STORE and FIND_VALUE use the connection transport with a 2000 ms deadline; loss does not apply to it. K = 10; α = 3 unless varied. A failed call removes the contact from the routing table (unless the table is down to K contacts), and every node pings its whole table on a jittered liveness interval.

Each configuration is repeated with several seeds; plots show the mean over seeds and a band of ±1 standard deviation of the per-seed means; tables give mean, variance and std.

Lookup scalability

Probes (FIND_NODE RPCs sent) per node lookup as N doubles. A lookup that queries until the K closest it knows have all answered sends at least min(N-1, K) probes, so the curve has a floor at K; past that, Kademlia predicts growth of O(log N), at most about log2N hops, each hop costing up to α probes. Bands are ±1 std over seeds.

What the numbers mean

  • From N = 16 to N = 16384 the network grows 1024× (10 doublings), but a lookup only needs 5.9 more probes (10.0 → 15.9, 0.59 per doubling) and 2.26 more hops (0.23 per doubling, against 1 per doubling for log₂N).
  • Hops stay far under log₂N (log₂16384 = 14.0, measured 2.26): each reply carries up to K = 10 contacts from the bucket nearest the target, so one hop usually gains several bits of shared prefix, not one.
  • The floor: a lookup only stops once its K closest candidates have all answered, so it can never send fewer than min(N−1, K) probes; at N ≤ 11 that is the whole network.
  • Against the runs before bucket refresh on join (--baseline), at N = 16384: 17.01 → 15.93 probes, 2.60 → 2.26 hops and 74 → 116 contacts per routing table (theirs → the main runs').

Lookup reliability vs packet loss

The network is built and the values stored on a lossless wire; then every datagram (request or reply) is dropped independently with probability p while the lookups run. An RPC is sent up to 5 times, 200 ms apart, and counts as answered if any reply arrives before the last wait ends, so a single RPC succeeds with probability 1 − (1 − (1−p)²)5. FIND_VALUE and STORE go over the connection transport, which loss does not touch, so a value lookup can fail only in its node-lookup phase.

What the numbers mean

  • Measured in a network of N = 5000 nodes. Each datagram is lost independently with probability p, and an RPC needs a request and its reply to both survive: one send gets through with probability (1−p)², and with 5 sends the call succeeds with 1 − (1 − (1−p)²)⁵.
  • Value lookups stay at ≥ 99% success up to p = 0.6, where a single send gets through only 16.0% of the time and a whole RPC 58.2%. A lookup sends 10–20 probes, needs only some of them to answer, and has K = 10 replicas to find; FIND_VALUE itself goes over the connection, which loss does not touch.
  • Node lookups (which must find one specific node) drop below 50% at p = 0.75, where a single RPC still succeeds 27.6% of the time: finding one exact node needs the one path to it to work, finding a value needs any of K.
  • Time pays first: at p = 0.5 lookups still succeed but take 38× longer (65 → 2469 ms median), since every lost datagram costs a 200 ms resend wait. At very high loss the time flattens near ⌈K/α⌉ s = 4 s: almost every probe times out after the full 1 s, and the lookup works through its ~K starting contacts α = 3 at a time without learning new ones.

Time between request and response vs latency

Every datagram is delayed by a fixed one-way latency L, so a reply takes 2L. The transport resends after 200 ms of silence and gives up after 5 sends (1 s total). Expect RTT ≈ 2L while 2L < 200 ms; past that each request is resent before its reply can arrive (wasted traffic, same answer), and once 2L passes 1 s no reply can arrive in time and every datagram RPC fails.

What the numbers mean

  • Measured in a network of N = 5000 nodes, built at ≤ 5 ms latency and switched to L for the measure phase, so the network exists even at latencies it could not have formed under.
  • A reply takes 2L. The transport resends every 200 ms of silence, so once 2L exceeds 200 ms (L > 100 ms) every request is sent at least twice before its reply can arrive: duplicate traffic, same answer. A send only helps if its reply lands by the time the call gives up at 1 s, so only ⌊(1 s − 2L) / 200 ms⌋ + 1 of the 5 sends count. At L = 500 ms that is exactly one, the first, whose reply is due at the deadline itself and usually just makes it; past 500 ms none do and every datagram RPC fails, however reliable the network.

Probes and lookup time vs α

α is how many FIND_NODEs a lookup keeps in flight. Expect: more α means more probes, since the lookup speculatively queries nodes a sequential lookup would have skipped once something closer turned up, but fewer rounds, so lower latency, with diminishing returns once α approaches K (the lookup can never have more than K useful candidates). Under loss, a larger α also hides timeouts: other queries make progress while one waits out its resends.

What the numbers mean

  • At loss 0 (N = 5000): going from α = 1 to α = 10 costs 18.1 more probes (11.4 → 29.5, +158%) and buys a 73% faster lookup (143 → 39 ms); α = 1 → 3 alone cuts the time by 55%.
  • At loss 0.3 (N = 5000): going from α = 1 to α = 10 costs 12.8 more probes (11.5 → 24.3, +111%) and buys a 57% faster lookup (2357 → 1010 ms); α = 1 → 3 alone cuts the time by 47%.
  • Why it flattens: a lookup ends once its K = 10 closest candidates have answered, so with α queries per round it needs at least ⌈K/α⌉ rounds to confirm them; that is 10 rounds at α = 1 but 1 round at α ≥ 10. Past that, extra parallel queries only hit nodes that turn out not to be among the closest: more probes, no fewer rounds.

Lookup reliability vs churn

During a paced measurement window, c random nodes per second leave (stop answering, without notice) and as many new ones join. Values are stored once before churn. Without republishing, a value disappears once every node holding it has left: a node survives t seconds with probability about e−ct/N, so a value with h holders is still available with probability 1 − (1 − e−ct/N)h. With republishing every T seconds, each surviving holder copies the value back onto the current K closest, so a value is lost only if all K holders leave within one interval, (1 − e−cT/N)K per interval. Lookups can also fail while the value exists, when routing tables still point at departed nodes; the liveness interval decides how long those linger.

What the numbers mean

  • With churn c = 25 nodes/s in N = 5000 nodes (0.5% of the network per second), as many nodes as the whole network have been replaced every 3 min 20 s (N/c). Because a leaving node is drawn from everyone alive, newcomers included, that point only means 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of the nodes are new; half of them are new after N·ln2/c = 2 min 19 s, and by the end of the 10 min window 95% have been replaced.
  • Value availability at the end of the window, by model: without republishing a value is gone once all K = 10 of its holders have left, 1 − (1 − e^(−cD/N))^K; republishing every T s re-copies it onto the current K closest, so it is lost only if all K leave within one interval, (1 − e^(−cT/N))^K per interval. The table uses T = 60 s.
  • Lookups can fail even while the value exists: routing tables keep pointing at departed nodes until a failed call or a liveness round removes them, and each probe to a dead node costs the full 1 s retry budget.

Heatmaps: latency × packet loss

N = 2000 nodes; every combination of one-way latency (0 to 600 ms) and loss (0 to 0.8), 3 seeds each. The RPC model here is 1 − (1 − (1−p)²)u, where u = ⌊(1 s − 2L) / 200 ms⌋ + 1 is how many of the 5 sends can still get a reply back by the time the call gives up (0 once 2L > 1 s).

What the numbers mean

  • Measured RPC success differs from the model by at most 0.090, at L = 200 ms and p = 0.5 (mean absolute difference 0.010 over the grid).
  • The gaps are at L = 100, 200, 300 ms, where 2L is a multiple of 200 ms: one send's reply falls due at the very instant the call gives up. The model counts such a tie as won. Which really comes first depends on the scheduling delay built up between that send and the deadline: none when it is the last send (a coin toss), more for each earlier one, so the tie at L = 400 and 500 ms, on the 2nd and 1st send, is reliably won, and at 100–300 ms it is lost some of the time, which is why measured success sits a little below the model there.
  • Two cliffs: along loss the success falls smoothly, as (1−p)² per send; along latency it drops in steps, one per 100 ms of L, each step one fewer send whose reply can arrive in time, down to zero once L passes 500 ms.

N = 2000 nodes over a 5 min window, liveness every 10 s; every combination of churn rate and republish interval (3600 s means no republish inside the window), 3 seeds each.

What the numbers mean
